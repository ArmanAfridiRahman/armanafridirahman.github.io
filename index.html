Before the work, a note

I would rather prove something is faster than assume it.

I am a software engineer from Dhaka. I spent about three years building production web applications in PHP and Laravel, growing from intern to mid-level developer and working directly with clients and remote teams along the way.

What I care about most is how software behaves under pressure: limited servers, tight budgets, failing networks, real money moving through the system. I look for the fast path, the lean query, and the integration that recovers when something goes wrong.

I use AI as a tool to move faster, and I read, test and profile everything it produces. The decisions that decide quality (the data model, the queries, the architecture, what ships) stay with me. I also build the surrounding structure: sub-agent setups, plugins and development patterns that make AI agents reliable for the work they are given.

The pages that follow describe the kinds of systems I have built. Client work is described by the problem and the approach, without client names, and the interactive pieces are illustrations with invented numbers.

No. 01Messaging

Dispatching under limits

Bulk email, SMS and WhatsApp, sent from a server that honestly isn’t up to it. The job still has to get done.

I have worked on SaaS systems that send campaigns in bulk across email, SMS and WhatsApp. The interesting constraint is not the sending. It is the machine doing the sending. A small server that is asked to push everything at once does not fail loudly: it drops work, and nobody notices until the numbers come back wrong.

So the focus is on delivering as much as possible inside the limits that exist: shape the work to what the server can carry, keep retries from becoming a second storm, and finish the job reliably. The same thinking covers scheduling, list handling and gateway integration for SMTP and SMS.

the payslip

Management apps that understand that every employee’s time is different, and turn it into a payslip people can trust.

I have built management applications that handle employee-specific time and generate payslips. Hours, rates, overtime and deductions are not one rule for everyone, so the system has to hold each person’s rules and still produce a clean, explainable result at the end of the month.

Access is part of the design. Staff, managers and admins see what they should and nothing more, which in a payroll-adjacent system is as important as the arithmetic.

Tables, menus and the order

Restaurant management built as SaaS: every table has its own QR code, every guest orders from a browser tab, and the admin controls all of it from one place.

I have worked on restaurant management systems built as multi-tenant SaaS, where each restaurant has its own menus, floor plan, schedules and staff tiers. Menus build up over months, added to by different people at different times. The system sorts them into clear categories, and lets an admin rename, reprice, reorder or switch off anything without calling a developer.

The floor is modelled table by table. A guest scans the QR code on their table and the menu opens in a browser tab, with no app to install. The order is tied to that table: it is marked as booked, staff see exactly what was ordered and where it goes, and the bill closes against the same table at the end.

Alongside these I have built note-keeping and money-management apps, where the same rule holds. Where money moves, callbacks that arrive late or twice are treated as normal, and the books are reconciled at the end.

    No. 04Ecommerce SaaS

    Checkout
    to the shelf

    Ecommerce platforms where one checkout talks to several payment gateways, and every paid order turns into a pick from a real warehouse shelf.

    I have built ecommerce systems, also delivered as SaaS with an admin who controls the catalogue, prices, gateways and stock. Customers pay in different ways, so the checkout integrates multiple payment gateways. Each one fails in its own way, so an order that times out on one can move to another without being charged twice, and duplicate callbacks are recognised and ignored.

    Warehouse management was a large part of the work. Stock is reserved when an order is placed, picked from a specific bin and packed for dispatch, and low stock triggers a restock before a product runs out. The stock count on screen has to match the shelf.

    Every order is one dot you can follow. It leaves the storefront, pays through one of four gateways, is picked from a warehouse bin, packed, loaded onto a van and dropped at the customer’s door.           Agents, plugins and patterns

          I do not just prompt AI. I build the structure around it: sub-agents with focused jobs, carefully crafted plugins, and development patterns that make the results dependable.

          Using AI well is an engineering problem, not a typing shortcut. I use sub-agents to split work into focused, separate jobs, so each one has a clear task and a clean context instead of one agent juggling everything at once.

          I make plugins for agents: packaged instructions, tools and guardrails that give an agent what it needs for a specific kind of work. And I design the development patterns around them, covering how a task is broken down, what is delegated, and how the result is checked before it ships.

          The point is the same as everywhere else on these pages: a system that is reliable and efficient, with an engineer who understands it staying in charge.
